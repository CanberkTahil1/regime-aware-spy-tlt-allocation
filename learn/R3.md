# R3 — The regime layer (L6–L8)

## In two sentences
On 1990–2019, at the same risk, none of the three regime layers improved on the layer before it: moving TLT to cash
when stocks and bonds stop hedging (L6) cost −0.08 Sharpe [−0.23, +0.07], the 10-month trend rule (L7) another −0.17
[−0.42, +0.08], and the drawdown brake (L8) nothing measurable. Each did what it was built for in its own crisis — L6
saved 5.9 pp in 1994, L7 saved 11.8 pp in the GFC and 10.0 pp in the dot-com bust — and paid for it everywhere else.

## Why these choices
- D3.1 (advisor): when the 126-day correlation is > 0 at month-end, TLT goes to bills; SPY is not topped up.
- D3.2 (advisor): quantkit's Faber rule on the same total-return series; a "trend only" row separates the layers.
- D3.3 (advisor): quantkit's drawdown brake with its own defaults, tested by `sizing.judge`.
- D3.4 (advisor): placebos shuffle each gate's monthly decisions in 12-month blocks; 100 seeds give a percentile.
- D3.5 (advisor): every comparison at matched risk, with intervals on Sharpe and on drawdown.
- D3.6 (advisor): HMM / jump gates, min-CDaR and max-div built; v1's overlays and the GBM row moved to R4.

## The one number to remember
**−0.08 excess Sharpe [−0.23, +0.07]**: L6 (the repo's namesake, the correlation regime) vs its parent at matched
risk, daily, development 1990–2019 (7,559 days). It sits at the 59th percentile of its own random-timing placebos.

## Concept check
- **Placebo test:** keep how often and for how long a gate is on, shuffle *when* (12-month blocks). If the real gate is
  no better than the shuffled ones, its timing adds nothing. Here every shuffled gate also costs about −0.10 Sharpe:
  in a 30-year bull market for both assets, *any* time out of the market costs return.
- **Matched-risk comparison:** ΔSharpe = SR(child − bill) − SR(k·parent + (1 − k)·bill − financing − bill), with
  k = σ_child / σ_parent. Without it, L6's lower drawdown would just be its lower risk (7.1% vs 8.1% vol).

## Interview questions
1. **Your strategy is named after the correlation regime. Does it work?** In the development sample it did exactly
   what it was designed to do once: 1994, when bonds and stocks fell together, it saved 5.9 pp. Otherwise, it sat in
   bills during long positive-correlation spells (the 1990s) while bonds rallied, for −0.08 Sharpe at matched risk.
   2022 is the case for it, and that is in the test window, which isn't opened until R5.
2. **Trend following cut your GFC loss by 12 points. Why not keep it?** Because the same rule lost in 1994, 2011,
   2015–16 and Q4 2018, when it sold dips that recovered or dropped the bond hedge right before it paid. Over 30 years
   the net is −0.17 Sharpe, and its own drawdowns are no smaller at equal risk (CDaR ratio 1.14). Judging a crash rule
   by its best crisis is selection.
3. **How do you know a regime gate isn't just "less risk"?** Two controls: compare at matched volatility, and compare
   against the same gate with its timing shuffled. Here the gates failed both: no better than the parent at equal
   risk, and inside the distribution of their shuffled copies (36th–86th percentile).

## What could be wrong
The sample is one long bond bull market (yields fell from ~8% to ~2%), which is exactly when moving bonds to cash
hurts most. So "L6 doesn't help" may be a property of 1990–2019, not of the idea. 2022 (positive correlation, both
assets falling) is the counter-case, and it sits in the unopened test window. The 25-year bond proxy also exaggerates
duration relative to many real allocations, and it cannot be checked against TLT before 2002, so L6's one win (1994)
rests on the proxy construction alone. Three zoo rows (v1's overlays, the GBM row) were not built; that deferral is
yours to accept (O3.1).
